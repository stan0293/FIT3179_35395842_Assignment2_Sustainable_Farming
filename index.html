Growing Tomorrow: Sustainable Farming in Australia

Explore how Australian farms use water, manage soil and address greenhouse gas emissions, and how these practices are changing over time.

Section 1: Australia’s Farming Landscape

Agricultural uses: Livestock production, cropping and horticulture.

Non-agricultural uses: Nature conservation, forests and plantations, urban and other intensive uses, and water.

Farming’s extensive footprint makes sustainable practices important

Agriculture occupies over half of the total mapped area across the 57 NRM regions, approximately 426.6 million hectares. This extensive footprint makes the way farms manage land an important part of Australia’s environmental sustainability.

The national total also hides regional differences. The next map shows how agricultural land share varies across regions, while the bar chart breaks down the broad land use categories.

WA: Western Australia; NT: Northern Territory; SA: South Australia; QLD: Queensland; NSW: New South Wales; VIC: Victoria; TAS: Tasmania; ACT: Australian Capital Territory. Dark outlines are derived from the mapped NRM regions grouped by state or territory.

Farming occupies different shares of regional landscapes

Agriculture accounts for a high share of the mapped area in many inland Queensland and New South Wales regions, while Tasmania and northern Cape York have lower shares.

A higher share means more of that region is used for agriculture. It does not indicate more sustainable farming or greater environmental impact.

Most agricultural land is used to raise animals

Livestock production occupies the largest share of agricultural land, followed by cropping and horticulture.

Cropping refers to growing field crops such as wheat, barley and canola. Horticulture includes growing fruit, vegetables, nuts and ornamental plants.

These shares describe how agricultural land is used. They do not tell us which farming activity is more sustainable.

Section 2: Water Use and Conservation

Circle area represents irrigation water applied to crops and pastures. GL means gigalitres. One gigalitre equals one billion litres. Symbols are positioned approximately within each state or territory. Values for the Northern Territory and ACT are not published.

Irrigation water supports crops and livestock pastures

New South Wales records the largest published volume of irrigation water applied, followed by Queensland and Victoria.

Irrigation supplies water to crops, horticultural plants and pastures grown for livestock. These figures exclude rainfall and do not represent all water used on farms.

Higher volumes do not necessarily indicate inefficient water use. They can reflect differences in irrigated area, crop types and weather conditions.

Irrigated area and application rate explain different water use profiles

Rice has the highest water application rate among the categories shown. Grazing pastures and cereals have the largest irrigated area and receive the greatest total volume.

Total irrigation volume depends on both the area watered and the amount applied per hectare. An activity can therefore receive a large total volume without having the highest application rate.

Application rates alone do not measure sustainability. Crop needs, rainfall and growing conditions also affect irrigation requirements.

Reported water management responses vary across states

Western Australia and the Northern Territory have the highest reported adoption of increased water storage. South Australia has the highest reported adoption of water efficient varieties.

Water efficient varieties and additional storage address different aspects of water availability. Storage can support preparedness for shortages, but does not necessarily reduce water consumption.

These are survey estimates for broadacre and dairy farms, excluding horticulture. They measure reported practice use, not actual water savings.

Section 3: Soil Management

Darker cells indicate a higher percentage of farms reporting the practice. Farms may use several practices, so percentages should not be added together. The survey covers broadacre and dairy farms, excluding horticulture.

Soil management practices differ between farming industries

Stubble retention is especially common among wheat and other crop farms and mixed livestock and cropping farms. Soil testing is reported widely across the surveyed industries.

Cover crops and controlled traffic are reported less often than soil testing in every industry shown. These differences can reflect how relevant each practice is to the farming system.

Adoption does not directly measure soil condition. A lower percentage should not automatically be interpreted as less sustainable farming.

Section 4: Emissions and Sustainability

Percentages represent farms reporting each barrier. Farms may report several barriers, so percentages should not be added together. The survey covers broadacre and dairy farms, excluding horticulture.

Uncertainty is the most commonly reported barrier

Uncertainty is reported more frequently than limited information or lack of knowledge in every farming industry shown.

These findings suggest that efforts to support emissions reduction need to address farmers’ uncertainty as well as their information and knowledge needs.

The chart measures perceived barriers. It does not show emissions volumes or prove that addressing a barrier will lead to emissions reductions.
