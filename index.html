Understanding agricultural land uses

  • Native grazing: Livestock graze naturally occurring vegetation.
  • Modified pastures: Livestock graze improved or altered pastures.
  • Irrigated pastures: Pastures receive water through irrigation.
  • Dryland cropping: Field crops rely on rainfall.
  • Irrigated cropping: Field crops receive additional irrigation water.
  • Dryland horticulture: Fruits, vegetables and specialised crops rely on rainfall.
  • Irrigated horticulture: Horticultural crops receive additional irrigation water.
  • Intensive production: Concentrated plant or animal production, often in managed facilities.

NOTE: I independently grouped ABARES's 18 land-use categories into two groups based on their primary land-use definitions. Areas are shown in hectares (ha), thousands of hectares (K ha) or millions of hectares (M ha).

Source: ABARES, Land use of Australia 2010–11 to 2020–21, Version 7.1. Australia_PA worksheet, closing stock 2020–21.

In 2020–21, agriculture occupied 54.5% of Australia's total land, with native vegetation grazing accounting for 74.3% of agricultural land.

This highlights Australia's extensive grazing-based farming landscape, where livestock feed on naturally occurring vegetationPlants growing naturally in an area, rather than planted pasture or crops..

With agriculture covering more than half of the country, sustainable farming practices are essential for managing land, conserving water and reducing environmental impacts.

Over a decade, Australia's agricultural total footprint barely changed. Between 2010–11 and 2020–21, agricultural land increased by just 0.26%, or 1.092 million hectares.

However, this stability hides major changes. Dryland croppingGrowing crops using rainfall rather than irrigation. expanded by 9.774 million hectares, while modified pasturesGrasslands planted or improved to provide feed for grazing livestock. declined by 8.732 million hectares.

Although these changes largely offset each other, they may have different environmental implications. Expanding cropping can affect soil health and erosion risks, depending on farming methods.

Across Australia, irrigation patterns also differ considerably between farming activities.

In 2020–21, grazing pasturesIrrigated grasslands used to feed livestock. covered the largest irrigated area (495,779 ha) and received the most irrigation water overall (1,429 GL).

In contrast, rice had the highest application rate (11.94 ML/ha) despite covering a much smaller area. This may partly reflect traditional rice cultivation, which often involves flooded fields.

These differences highlight why sustainable irrigation requires different water-management approaches across different farming activities.

Managing water goes beyond how much farms use. It also depends on the practices they adopt.

In 2021, increasing on-farm water storageExpanding water-storage capacity for farming, particularly during dry periods. was more common among Australian farms (54%) than using water-efficient crop or pasture varietiesVarieties selected to better manage water needs. (40%).

Western Australia (65%) and New South Wales (63%) recorded the highest adoption of increased water storage, while New South Wales and Victoria (43% each) led in water-efficient varieties.

These practices can help farms manage water availability during dry periods and better meet crop water needs, supporting more sustainable farming.

Understanding Water Sources

  • Irrigation channels or pipelines: Water delivered through irrigation supply networks.
  • Rivers, creeks or lakes: Water taken directly from these surface-water sources.
  • Groundwater: Underground water accessed through bores or wells.
  • On-farm dams or tanks: Water drawn from storage located on the farm.
  • Recycled or reused water: Previously used water supplied for use again.
  • Reticulated mains supply: Water delivered through the local water-supply pipes, like town tap water.

NOTE: Figures show water sourced by farms, not water saved or stored. Changes can reflect rainfall, drought and water availability. One gigalitre (GL) equals one billion litres.

Source: ABS, Water Use on Australian Farms, 2015–16 to 2020–21. Volumes converted to GL and rounded.

Drought context: Australian Bureau of Meteorology, Climate of the 2018–19 financial year and Annual climate statement 2019.

While many Australian farms reported increasing their water-storage capacity, on-farm dams and tanks were only one of several water sources supporting agriculture.

Water sourced from irrigation channels and rivers fell sharply during the severe 2017–2019 drought, before recovering in 2020–21 as rainfall improved. During this period, groundwater remained an important supporting source, suggesting that farms relied on multiple water sources when surface water was less available.

Even so, irrigation channels, rivers and groundwater remained the dominant sources, while recycled water contributed relatively little. This suggests that Australian farming still depended mainly on conventional water supplies.

Overall, the pattern shows that water-storage practices may help farms prepare for dry conditions, but sustainable farming also depends on managing different water sources carefully as water availability changes.

Understanding Soil Management

Farming Industries
  • Wheat and other crops: Farms mainly growing crops such as wheat and barley.
  • Mixed livestock and cropping: Farms growing crops and raising livestock.
  • Dairy: Farms raising cows mainly for milk production.
  • Beef: Farms raising cattle mainly for meat production.
  • Sheep: Farms raising sheep for wool or meat.
  • Sheep and beef: Farms raising both sheep and cattle.
Soil-Management Practices
  • Soil testing: Checking soil nutrients and condition to guide farming decisions.
  • Ground cover: Keeping vegetation or plant material on soil to reduce erosion.
  • Stubble retention: Leaving crop stalks and residues on the ground after harvesting.
  • Cover crops: Growing plants mainly to protect or improve soil between crops.
  • Incorporated organic matter: Mixing compost, manure or plant material into soil.
  • Controlled traffic: Keeping farm machinery on designated tracks to reduce soil compaction.

NOTE: Percentages are survey estimates of farms reporting each practice during the three years to 2023–24. Farms may report multiple practices. Higher adoption does not necessarily mean healthier soil.

Source: ABARES, A Survey of Emissions and Natural Resource Management Practices in Australia, 2023–24. NRM practices worksheet; national observations by industry.

Beyond conserving water, maintaining healthy soil is essential for sustainable farming, helping retain moisture, support crop growth and reduce erosion.

Soil testingChecking soil nutrients and condition to guide farming decisions. was the leading practice in three of the six farming industries and was widely reported across most industries, highlighting its relevance to soil and nutrient management.

In contrast, stubble retentionLeaving crop stalks and residues on the ground after harvesting. was particularly common among crop-producing farms, reaching 97% in wheat and other crop farms and 82% in mixed livestock and cropping farms.

These patterns show that sustainable soil management is not one-size-fits-all, as different farming activities benefit from different soil-management practices.

Soil organic carbon supports sustainable farming by helping retain moisture, maintain soil fertility and support plant growth.

Between 2010 and 2018, Australia's agricultural land showed a slight overall increase in modelled soil organic carbon, one indicator relevant to soil health.

However, regional differences remain, with some areas gaining soil carbon while others experienced declines, showing that improvements were not uniform across the country.

Overall, these findings suggest that maintaining and improving soil carbon is important for sustainable farming, while continued attention to local soil conditions remains essential.

Beyond conserving water and protecting soil, reducing greenhouse gas emissions is another important part of sustainable farming.

Australia's agricultural emissions fell by 15.8% between 1989–90 and 2023–24, although the decline was uneven, with several periods of increase.

Despite this reduction, methane accounted for 83.3% of agricultural emissions in 2023–24, largely associated with livestock digestion and manure management.

These findings show that targeting major emission sources, particularly livestock-related methane, is important for making Australian farming more sustainable.

Understanding Agricultural Emission Sources

  • Livestock digestion: Cattle and sheep release methane when digesting food.
  • Manure management: Stored or handled animal waste releases methane and nitrous oxide. Nitrous oxide from grazing waste is counted under soils.
  • Agricultural soils: Nitrogen from fertilisers, animal waste and plant residues can release nitrous oxide.
  • Rice cultivation: Plant material decomposing in flooded rice fields releases methane.
  • Burning crop residues: Burning crop leftovers releases methane and nitrous oxide.
  • Liming and urea application: Applying lime to reduce soil acidity or urea fertiliser releases carbon dioxide.

The previous chart showed that methane has consistently dominated Australia's agricultural emissions over time. But which farming activities are responsible?

Throughout the years, livestock digestion has remained a major contributor to agricultural emissions. In 2023–24, it was the largest emissions source (77.7%), mainly releasing methane through the digestive processes of cattle and sheep. Meanwhile, agricultural soils were the main contributor to nitrous oxide emissions, while liming and urea application contributed to carbon dioxide emissions.

These findings highlight the need for targeted emissions-reduction strategies, particularly addressing livestock-related methane and soil-related nitrous oxide, rather than relying on a single approach.

After identifying the major sources of agricultural emissions, the next question is whether farmers know how much their own farms emit.

In 2023–24, only 7% of broadacre and dairy farms knew their net emissions, while 12% wanted to know, revealing a significant gap in emissions awareness.

Although 18% planned a new natural-resource or emissions-management activity, these intentions do not guarantee lower emissions. Greater awareness could be an important first step towards managing them.

While some farmers intend to manage their emissions, turning intentions into action can be challenging.

In 2023–24, uncertainty was the most commonly reported barrier (59%) among Australian broadacre and dairy farms, followed by limited information and lack of knowledge (47% each). Uncertainty also ranked highest across all six farming industries.

These findings highlight that clearer information and practical guidance are important in helping farmers make informed decisions towards reducing agricultural emissions.

So, is Australian farming becoming more sustainable?

Australian farming shows progress towards sustainability, but improvements are uneven.

Agricultural land use shifted between 2010–11 and 2020–21, while the overall agricultural footprint remained largely stable.

The 2021 and 2023–24 surveys show practices supporting water conservation and soil protection. These practices must therefore suit each industry's local conditions.

Emissions declined between 1989–90 and 2023–24. Soil carbon increased slightly overall during 2010–2018, but some regions experienced declines.

Livestock methane, low emissions awareness and uncertainty remain challenges to further progress.

Therefore, greater sustainability requires suitable practices and informed decisions, with measured benefits for water, soil and emissions.
